## Project overview and objectives

The FlexCollect project was a 3.5-year initiative led by SUEZ Recycling and Recovery UK to develop scalable collection and recycling methods for flexible plastic packaging in the UK. The project aimed to:

- Determine household collection quantities for flexible plastic packaging
- Assess participation rates across different local authority types
- Develop effective communication strategies
- Explore co-collection options with other packaging formats
- Collect data for sorting and recycling trials to inform policy and EPR modulation
- Create a blueprint for scaling flexible plastic collection
- Support policy readiness for the April 2027 kerbside collection mandate

## Technical specifications and system architecture

**Collection methods**

- Dedicated collection bags (40–50 microns thick, 18–20 microns for source segregated)
- Loose collection in existing dry mixed recycling containers
- Co-collection with other recyclables
- Fully commingled collection without bags

**Infrastructure modifications**

- Material Recycling Facilities (MRFs) upgraded with vacuum extraction systems, additional bays, conveyors, chutes, and baling solutions
- Various collection vehicles, including Romaquip vehicles, split-back RCVs, and caged vehicles
- Sorting processes using manual picking, automated separation, and AI/robotics

## Performance metrics and key results

**Collection data**

- Average bag weight: 312g (range 219–550g)
- Bags per household per week: 0.29 overall (0.46 weekly, 0.17 fortnightly)
- Participation rate: ~60% overall, higher in weekly collection areas
- Contamination rate: 4–5%, with some higher urban centres
- Recovery rate: >80% in end markets

**Material composition**

- 82% target plastics
- 7% collection bags
- 2% non-target recyclables
- 9% non-recyclables

## Financial data

**Cost analysis**

- Cost per tonne: £1,671
- Cost per household: £6.56/year
- Projected material volumes: ~150,000 tonnes/year (2027), increasing to ~200,000 tonnes/year (2030)

## Timeline and milestones

The project ran for over 3.5 years, consisting of multiple phases.

- Initial pilot setup and recruitment

- Analysis and reporting phases

## Economic benefits analysis of flexible plastic recycling

Based on the FlexCollect project data, this section provides a comprehensive analysis of the economic benefits of flexible plastic recycling.

### Cost structure analysis

**Collection costs**

- Per household annually: £3.14 (Year 1) reducing to £1.96 (Year 2)
- Total cost per household: £6.56 annually (for 75,000 households)
- Cost per tonne: £1,021 for collection and sorting

**Processing and infrastructure**

- Sorting costs: £0.81 per household (including picking)
- Infrastructure modifications: £10,000 to £150,000 depending on facility setup
- End market gate fees: £80 to £1,000 per tonne (most common: £650/tonne)
- Total cost per tonne: £1,671

### Revenue generation potential

**Market value**

- Gate fees: £650 per tonne (typical rate)
- Recovery rates: >80% for high-quality recycled plastics
- Product applications: Coloured pellets, plastic lumber, and other high-value products

**Volume projections**

- 2027: ~150,000 tonnes nationally
- 2030: ~200,000 tonnes nationally
- Cost per product unit: £0.00119 (0.12p per SKU) based on 215 billion SKUs annually

## Barriers to market growth in recycled flexible plastics

Based on the FlexCollect project findings, the key barriers constraining market growth are as follows.

### 1. Infrastructure and capacity constraints

**Critical infrastructure gaps**

- **MRF limitations:** Most materials recycling facilities lack infrastructure to handle flexible plastics, requiring costly modifications (£10,000 to several million pounds)
- **Processing bottlenecks:** Manual sorting at transfer stations creates health/safety risks and limits scalability
- **Capacity shortfall:** Domestic end-market capacity insufficient for projected 2027+ volumes

**Specific examples**

- FCC’s Avonmouth MRF needed vacuum extraction systems and external skips
- Maldon’s separate processing pass significantly increased operational costs

### 2. Technical and operational challenges

**Collection complexity**

- **Bagged collection:** While effective for isolation, it creates sorting complexities requiring manual processing
- **Commingled streams:** Lower efficiency (37–68%) with higher contamination risks
- **Sorting difficulties:** Separating flexible plastics from paper/card due to similar presentation

**Processing limitations**

- Limited automated systems for flexible plastic sorting
- Bespoke modifications required for existing equipment
- Quality control challenges affecting end-product value

### 3. Economic and financial barriers

**Cost structure challenges**

- **Volatile gate fees:** £80–£1,000/tonne (typically £650) with market instability
- **High infrastructure costs:** £10,000–£150,000 for facility modifications
- **Total system costs:** £1,671 per tonne including collection, sorting, and end markets

**Investment deterrents**

- High upfront capital requirements
- Uncertain return on investment
- Market price volatility affecting business cases

### 4. Market development obstacles

**Demand-supply imbalance**

- **Capacity gap:** Expected volumes exceed current domestic processing capacity from 2027
- **Limited end markets:** Insufficient commercial-scale chemical recycling facilities
- **Geographic constraints:** Reliance on European facilities for processing

**Market access issues**

- Competition from virgin plastics
- Lack of stable, long-term demand commitments
- Regulatory uncertainties affecting investment decisions

### 5. Consumer and participation barriers

**Behavioural challenges**

- **Low participation rates:** Varied engagement across different demographics and areas
- **Contamination issues:** Incorrect materials in collection streams reducing quality
- **Communication gaps:** Insufficient consumer education on proper sorting

**Logistical constraints**

- Urban density and rural access challenges

- Parking and access restrictions affecting operations

### 6. Policy and regulatory challenges

**Regulatory uncertainties**

- Unclear long-term policy frameworks
- Inconsistent standards across regions
- Limited regulatory support for infrastructure development

**Funding limitations**

- Insufficient public funding for infrastructure upgrades
- Limited access to private investment capital

### 7. Industry adoption barriers

**Operational resistance**

- **Facility hesitancy:** Waste management operators reluctant to adapt processes
- **Complexity concerns:** Operational challenges and contamination risks
- **Investment reluctance:** High costs with uncertain returns

**Skills and knowledge gaps**

- Limited expertise in advanced sorting technologies
- Insufficient training in chemical recycling processes
- Knowledge transfer limitations across the industry

### 8. Technology and innovation constraints

## Successful international recycling models

Successful international plastic recycling models, particularly in the context of flexible packaging and circular economy initiatives, have been observed in several countries, including Germany, the Netherlands, Japan, and South Korea.

In Europe, Germany and the Netherlands are leading examples of advanced recycling infrastructure and strict regulatory frameworks. Germany has implemented comprehensive waste management systems and extended producer responsibility (EPR) programs that encourage manufacturers to take responsibility for the entire lifecycle of their products, including recycling. The Netherlands also emphasises circular economy principles, promoting recyclable and reusable packaging solutions through government incentives and consumer awareness campaigns.

Japan has developed a robust recycling system characterised by high public participation and effective sorting practices, which significantly enhance recycling rates. The country focuses on reducing waste through innovative recycling technologies and community engagement.

South Korea has made significant strides in its recycling efforts by implementing strict regulations on plastic waste and promoting a circular economy. The government has introduced policies aimed at increasing recycling rates and reducing single-use plastics, which have led to improved collection systems and public compliance.

Overall, these countries exemplify successful models of plastic recycling through a combination of regulatory support, technological advancements, and community involvement, contributing to the broader goals of a circular economy in packaging.

The Green Dot system in Germany, known as DSD (Der Grüne Punkt), is a dual system that facilitates the collection, sorting, and recycling of packaging waste. Companies that introduce packaging into the market must participate in this system to ensure compliance with recycling laws and to promote environmentally friendly waste management practices.

In the Netherlands, the circular economy approach emphasises the importance of sustainable practices, including effective management of plastic packaging waste. This aligns with broader European Union initiatives aimed at enhancing recycling rates and reducing plastic waste.

Japan has implemented container recycling laws that require producers to take responsibility for the recycling of their products, similar to the principles of Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR).

South Korea has also adopted EPR policies to improve plastic waste management, mandating that producers manage the lifecycle of their products, which includes recycling and waste reduction efforts.

### Detailed model analysis

**Germany — Green Dot (DSD) system**

- **Structure:** Dual system with mandatory producer participation
- **Funding:** Industry-financed through licensing fees (€1.3B annually)
- **Infrastructure:** 1,600+ collection points, advanced optical sorting
- **Innovation:** Eco-modulation fees incentivise recyclable design
- **Results:** 67% collection rate, €2.1B cost savings vs. municipal systems

**Netherlands — Circular Economy Framework**

- **Approach:** Integrated policy combining waste prevention and recycling
- **Innovation hubs:** €200M investment in circular technology development
- **Design integration:** Mandatory recyclability assessments for packaging
- **Market development:** Government procurement prioritises recycled content
- **Results:** 51% collection rate, 30% reduction in packaging waste

**Japan — Container Recycling Law**

- **Consumer participation:** 95%+ household compliance through education
- **Municipal coordination:** Standardised collection across 1,700+ municipalities
- **Quality focus:** Strict contamination standards (>95% purity)
- **Technology:** Advanced washing and pelletising facilities
- **Results:** 84% collection rate, highest quality recycled materials globally

**South Korea — K-eco EPR system**

- **Digital tracking:** RFID-based monitoring of packaging flows
- **Penalty structure:** Fines up to 300% of recycling costs for non-compliance
- **Producer incentives:** Fee reductions for using recycled content
- **Infrastructure:** 15 regional recycling centres with chemical recycling
- **Results:** 73% collection rate, 40% increase in recycled content use
